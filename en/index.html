How Blazhevich became Blazhevich

A life in music · 1881–1942

From an orphan on a poor Smolensk farm to a professor at the Moscow Conservatory whose etudes are still assigned at American universities.

1881–1893Smolensk region

An orphan from the Tregubovka farm

Vladislav Mikhailovich Blazhevich was born on 3 (15) August 1881 at the Tregubovka farm in the Katyn district of Smolensk. His father Mikhail came from the Polish nobility. According to the researcher Andrey Kharlamov, in the year of his son’s birth the father was arrested for anti-government views and sentenced to twenty years of hard labour.[2]

His mother could not manage the household alone and died in 1887, when the boy was six. An uncle on his father’s side became his guardian. They lived in poverty: according to Kharlamov, the boy had no shoes of his own until he was twelve. His first instrument was an ocarina. Colleagues later said that these early losses made him withdrawn, but also gave him a strong will and the habit of relying only on himself.[2]

1893–1900Military bands

Twelve years old — and into the army

At twelve his uncle placed him as a band pupil with the 3rd Artillery Brigade in Smolensk. There he had his own clothes and his own instrument for the first time. He started on the euphonium but asked to switch to the trombone, which offered more chances to play solos and to get into a real orchestra. In 1899 he served in the Astrakhan Grenadier Regiment, and in 1900 he left the army to study.[2]

1900–1905Moscow

The Conservatory and Bork’s class

In 1900 Blazhevich entered the Moscow Conservatory in the trombone class of Christoph Bork. He later remembered his teacher as “a dry and pedantic teacher who liked discipline and demanded unquestioning fulfilment of his instructions.” Besides trombone he studied music theory, orchestration and piano, and persuaded the administration to let him attend lessons for other instruments to understand how they were taught and played. He later carried this broad training into his own teaching. He graduated in 1905.[1][2]

1905–1928Bolshoi Theatre

The Bolshoi, the war, Persimfans

After the Conservatory Blazhevich won a place in the Bolshoi Theatre orchestra. English Wikipedia, following Kharlamov, gives the 1906 season; Russian sources give 1905. Colleagues remembered him as strong-willed and kind, and an excellent section leader. He also played in the Moscow Brass Quartet with S. Loginov, A. Kolpinsky and A. Andrushkevich.[1][2][7]

During the First World War he was drafted as an assistant superintendent of a military hospital. He kept playing at the Bolshoi and composed at night. In 1916 he organised a benefit concert of his own works in memory of the fallen.[2]

In 1922–1932 he played in Persimfans, the First Conductorless Symphony Ensemble. In 1926, according to Russian Wikipedia, he organised and led the wind band of the Special Armed Detachment. He left the Bolshoi in 1928 as principal trombone; his last performance was Borodin’s Prince Igor. Colleagues thought retiring at 47 was too early.[2]

1920–1942Conservatory

Professor and author of methods

In 1920 the rector of the Moscow Conservatory, M. M. Ippolitov-Ivanov, invited Blazhevich to become professor of trombone and tuba. He founded the military bandmasters’ class, which later grew into the faculty of military conductors, and headed that department in 1929–1936. In the 1930s he also taught at the Gnesin school.[1][2]

As the Conservatory puts it, there were almost no methods, etudes or pieces for trombone and tuba in Russia at the time, and this “repertoire hunger” made Blazhevich write them himself. He wrote two trombone methods: an advanced school in clefs in 1925 and a beginners’ school with piano accompaniment in 1936. He also wrote a legato school, 26 sequences, 38 concert duets, 12 melodic and 24 virtuoso etudes, etudes for alto and bass trombone, and a tuba method and 70 studies for tuba. By his own account, he wrote the thirteen concertos to show all the capabilities of the trombone and to give students solo repertoire. Most of the concertos date from the 1920s; Nos. 11–13 were probably written in the 1930s.[1][3][6]

Over twenty-two years at the Conservatory he trained many trombonists and tuba players. His best pupils are named as V. A. Shcherbinin, soloist of the Bolshoi and Conservatory professor, and B. P. Grigoriev, professor at the Gnesin Institute.[1][7]

1920–1941Conductor and author

Goznak and the State Wind Orchestra of the USSR

According to the Krugosvet encyclopedia, from 1920 Blazhevich directed the wind band of the Goznak factory, and led it for many years.[8]

Blazhevich founded the State Wind Orchestra of the USSR himself. An order of the All-Union Committee for the Arts of 28 January 1937 appointed him its artistic director retroactively, from 1 January 1937. The orchestra was at its height in 1937–1939, under Blazhevich; after he left, the baton passed to B. P. Moshkov.[9]

In July 1941 almost the whole orchestra, about sixty musicians, enlisted in the 6th People’s Militia Division of Moscow’s Dzerzhinsky district. Within two or three months the musicians went missing in the Vyazma encirclement. The newspaper Vechernyaya Moskva is still searching for their names.[9]

Late in life he wrote a book on the methods of teaching the trombone. Kharlamov cites it as Methods, 1941; English Wikipedia gives 1948, probably the year of publication. There is also an Autobiography of 1939.[2][3]

1942Moscow

Last years and legacy

Blazhevich died in Moscow on 10 April 1942 and is buried at the Vvedenskoye Cemetery (plot 10). In 1980 the Moscow children’s music school for wind and percussion No. 80 was named after him. On 17 January 2011 the Moscow Conservatory held a concert for the 130th anniversary of his birth.[2]

What made him who he was

Editorial summary of the sources
  • Character. Orphaned early and raised in poverty, he learned to rely only on himself. Colleagues saw this as the basis of his success.
  • Early practice. From the age of twelve he played every day in military bands, first on euphonium, then on trombone.
  • Broad training. Strict Bork, plus theory, orchestration, piano and lessons for other instruments.
  • A top-level stage. More than twenty years at the Bolshoi and ten in Persimfans, where the ensemble holds together without a conductor.
  • A gap in the repertoire. There was nothing to teach from, so he wrote it himself: methods, etudes, duets, concertos.

This is the editors’ summary of the facts above, not a quotation from a source.

Where the sources disagree
  • Start at the Bolshoi. The Conservatory and Russian Wikipedia give 1905; Kharlamov and the Gnesin school give 1906.
  • Date of death. Usually 10 April 1942; the English Wikipedia infobox gives 11 April.
  • Publication years. The Conservatory dates the Legato School to 1926, the duets to 1928 and the Sequences to 1938. Kharlamov gives 1924, 1926 and 1924; classON dates the School for the Slide Trombone to 1935 and part one of the legato school to 1938. Some dates may refer to composition, others to publication. English Wikipedia dates the first method to 1916, Kharlamov to 1925.
  • Tuba method. According to Kharlamov, the School for Contrabass Tuba was published by Muzgiz in 1937. The Conservatory lists the tuba school among post-war publications.
  • Raichman and Blazhevich. English Wikipedia says they studied together “in the early 1940s.” This conflicts with other data: according to Yeo, Raichman worked in Boston from 1927, and according to Kharlamov he took Blazhevich’s music out of Russia in 1926.[2][3][4]

Blazhevich in America

Recognition overseas

Jacob Raichman, the bridge

Jacob (Jacques) Raichman (1892–1982) was born in Vitebsk, the son of an army bandmaster. He graduated in 1911 from the St. Petersburg Conservatory in the class of P. N. Volkov, then played at the Bolshoi, where he met Blazhevich and studied with him for a time.[4]

Serge Koussevitzky hired him for the Boston Symphony reportedly without an audition. With the Russian immigration quota full, Raichman arrived via Havana in January 1927. He became co-principal around 1928 and principal after Joannès Rochut left in 1930, until 1955.[4]

He brought Blazhevich’s 1925 method, the Sequences, Concert Duets, Legato School and Concerto No. 9 from Russia, taught from them in Boston and wrote to Blazhevich for more. In 1948 Leeds Music published his edition of the 1925 method as Clef Studies for Trombone.[3][4]

A complicated legacy

According to Kharlamov, the first US editions (Concerto No. 2, Cundy-Bettoney, and Concert Piece No. 5, Belwin, both 1939) and many later ones appeared without permission and without royalties to his heirs, often with heavy editorial changes. From 2007 East-West International has published editions based on the originals with the family’s consent.[2][3]

Two Russian trombone schools

Teacher → pupil

The Moscow and St. Petersburg schools developed side by side, and Jacob Raichman linked both of them with America. Below: who studied with whom. Gaps in the sources are marked in the text.

Moscow

  1. 1832–1916 Christoph (Khristofor Ivanovich) Bork

    Born in Havelberg, Prussia. In 1861 he took Russian citizenship and joined the Bolshoi Theatre orchestra. Until 1882 he was principal trombone of the ballet orchestra; from 1875 he also played timpani, and from 1889 timpani only. Professor at the Conservatory from 1875, he taught trombone, tuba and percussion until his death and drew up a five-year trombone curriculum. Order of St. Stanislaus, 3rd class.[1][5]

    Pupils: V. M. Blazhevich, S. Diev, V. Derzhanovsky, K. Kupinsky, I. Piorkovsky, A. Sobolevsky, S. Chesnokov
  2. 1881–1942 Vladislav Mikhailovich Blazhevich

    Studied with Bork in 1900–1905; Conservatory professor from 1920. His methods and etudes became the teaching foundation for the next generations.

    Pupils: V. A. Shcherbinin, B. P. Grigoriev, K. G. Moreinis, G. M. Voronin, and J. Raichman (for a time)
  3. 1896–1963 Vladimir Shcherbinin (V. A. Shcherbinin)

    Pupil of Blazhevich, soloist of the Bolshoi Theatre orchestra and professor at the Moscow Conservatory. According to the Gnesin school, he created his own system of daily trombone practice. A. K. Lebedev, who founded a separate tuba class in 1950, graduated from his class.[1][7]

  4. from 1944 B. P. Grigoriev

    Pupil of Blazhevich and soloist of the Bolshoi Theatre orchestra. From 1944 he taught trombone and tuba at the newly founded Gnesin Institute and became a professor there. So Blazhevich’s school continued at two Moscow institutions at once.[7]

    Pupils: V. Zdorov, V. Dosadin, V. Esipov, K. Lokalin, M. Chepkoi
  5. later Shcherbinin’s pupils

    V. B. Batashov, Conservatory professor; M. M. Zeinalov (1917–1993), Bolshoi soloist, Conservatory professor and editor of the 1985 collection of Blazhevich etudes; K. M. Ladilov, professor at the Gnesin Institute. P. I. Chumakov also taught trombone at the Conservatory, and later Batashov and A. T. Skobelev.

St. Petersburg — Leningrad

  1. 1831–1909 Franz de Paula Türner

    Austrian; from 1855 in the Imperial theatre orchestras of St. Petersburg. Taught the Conservatory’s trombone and tuba class in 1870–1909 and wrote elementary methods for brass (1877, 1878).

    Pupils: P. N. Volkov, A. F. Kazbiryuk, K. Kreek, R. Kull
  2. 1877–1933 Pyotr Naumovich Volkov

    Principal trombone of the Mariinsky Theatre orchestra (1899–1933), where he played alongside Reiche; Conservatory professor (1917–1933). Jacob Raichman graduated from his class in 1911, and in the early 1930s Kozlov and Korshunov studied with him at the workers’ faculty. He died on 22 February 1933; Reiche took over the class (associate professor from 1933, acting professor from 1935).[4][5] Patronymic and dates corrected after the St. Petersburg Conservatory encyclopedia.

    Pupils: J. A. Raichman (graduated 1911), A. A. Kozlov and N. S. Korshunov (workers’ faculty), I. A. Gershkovich and others
  3. 1878–1946 Eugen (Evgeny Adolfovich) Reiche

    Born in Dresden; graduated from the Dresden Conservatory in trombone in 1896. Moved to St. Petersburg in 1897. In 1899 he won the bass trombone post at the Mariinsky Theatre under Nápravník, playing his own concerto, and stayed until 1941. Taught from 1906; at the Leningrad Conservatory from 1933, professor from 1940. Died in evacuation in Tashkent.[5]

    Pupils: A. A. Kozlov, N. S. Korshunov, I. M. Polyatskin (1st prize, All-Union competition 1941), L. A. Ignatiev (class of 1942)
  4. 1908–1992 Akim Alekseevich Kozlov

    From the Smolensk region, a band pupil of a railway regiment. Studied with Volkov, then with Reiche. From 1937 in the Leningrad Philharmonic orchestra, joining at the same time as Mravinsky. Taught at the Conservatory in 1946–1992.[5]

    Pupils: V. Venglovsky, B. Vinogradov, G. Danilov, A. Evtushenko, G. Strautman
    1910–1989 Nikolai Sergeevich Korshunov

    Also studied with Volkov, then with Reiche (graduated 1939). Soloist of the Leningrad Philharmonic orchestra in 1937–1972. Taught at the Conservatory from 1948, professor from 1972.[5]

    Pupils: V. Sumerkin, A. Gzhanyants, O. Korolev; tuba players N. Novikov, V. Galuzin
  5. 1926–1994 Victor Fyodorovich Venglovsky

    Graduated from the Conservatory under Kozlov in 1953; postgraduate study with the horn player M. N. Buyanovsky. In the Leningrad Philharmonic orchestra from 1950; taught at the Conservatory from 1966, professor from 1984. Author of the widely used Daily Exercises for Trombone. In 1974 he sent all 13 Blazhevich concertos to the United States.[3][5]

    Pupils: A. N. Morozov (professor at the Moscow Conservatory), V. V. Golikov and others

Editors’ observation: Moscow began with the Prussian trombonist Christoph Bork. St. Petersburg began with the Austrian Franz Türner (from 1870), followed by his pupil Pyotr Volkov and the German Eugen Reiche from Dresden. The branches intertwined: Raichman, a St. Petersburg graduate, studied with the Muscovite Blazhevich and took his music to America, and half a century later the Leningrader Venglovsky sent his concertos there too.

Thirteen trombone concertos

Solo repertoire

Written for his students, they are played without breaks between movements, with piano interludes linking them. Blazhevich modelled them on Ferdinand David’s Concertino, which he called the most successful work for trombone.[3][6]
